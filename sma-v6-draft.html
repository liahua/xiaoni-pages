The Parent Phase

Above about 60°C, nickel-titanium has a simple crystal structure called B2 — the same structure as cesium chloride.

Each unit cell is a cube with edge length a₀ = 3.15 Å. Titanium atoms sit at the corners. A nickel atom sits at the center.

This is the austenite phase. The metal is stiff, springy, and has one shape it wants to be.

Cool It Down

Below about 60°C, the cubic structure becomes unstable. The atoms want to rearrange into a lower-symmetry shape — B19′, a monoclinic structure.

The cube stretches along one axis, compresses along another, and shears. The new lattice parameters:

a = 2.889 Å   b = 4.120 Å   c = 4.622 Å   β = 96.8°

Drag the slider to watch the transformation happen.

Twelve Ways to Fall

The cubic cell has three axes it could choose as its compression direction. For each axis, the shear can go two ways. And the atoms inside can shuffle in two patterns.

3 × 2 × 2 = 12 lattice correspondence variants.

All twelve produce the same monoclinic structure — same lattice parameters, same angles. The only difference is which direction they fell.

Pick any two from the dropdown. Watch how they deform differently from the same starting cube.

Twins

When two adjacent regions of the crystal choose different variants, they need to stay connected — no gaps, no overlaps at the boundary.

This is only possible if the deformation on both sides of the boundary differs by a simple shear along that plane. When it works, they're called twins.

The mathematical condition: the matrix C₁⁻¹C₂ − I must have a zero eigenvalue. In other words:

det(C₁⁻¹C₂ − I) = 0

The eigenvector for that zero eigenvalue is the twin plane normal — the direction the boundary faces.

Not every pair of variants can twin. Try different combinations.
